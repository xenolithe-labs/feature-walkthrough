# Sources behind walkthrough-design

Opened and read on 2026-11-20. Each row says what the source says (a paraphrase, with short quotes where marked), which rule in [`SKILL.md`](SKILL.md) it supports, and how sure I am that I read it correctly and that it applies.

**How I read them.** "Full text" means I read the extracted text of the PDF myself. "Page via fetch" means a web page was returned through a summarising fetch tool, so the wording is the tool's rendering of the page, not raw HTML; I treat those as medium confidence even when the claim is simple. Quotes marked with quotation marks come from the text I read.

**Confidence:** High = read in full text, claim is plain. Medium = read via fetch, or the claim needs a step of inference. Low = secondary source or indirect support.

Section numbers in the "Used for" column are those of `SKILL.md`; `SKILL.md` points back here with the ids in the first column.

**Applicability warning.** Nearly all learning research below tests short lessons (minutes), with narrated animations or text and pictures, on students in laboratories. A stepped, multi-view, reader-paced explanation is close to that but not the same. Rules taken from it are hypotheses; the reader test (SKILL.md section 10) decides.

## Claims that are working practice or judgement, not research

Marked `[P]` in `SKILL.md`, no source: three to seven steps of one to five calls; about five things tracked at once; two or three sentences per call text; two levels of detail (L5 says "two levels" of interface features, so this one is borrowed by analogy); total length of 15 to 25 calls at about 15 seconds a call.

Untagged in `SKILL.md`, so craft judgement supported at most by general principles: the loop pattern of first, second and the one that differs; one new term per call and no forward references (L6, match with the reader's world); keeping layout still between calls (L2 on perception of change, indirect); three or four colour meanings; reserved example domains; choosing one valid order for concurrent events; distinct values; allocation of content between highlight, text, payload and intro.

## Where sources disagree or the evidence is thin

- **Animation versus stepped static graphics.** Tversky et al. found no reliable animation advantage once information and interactivity were equal. The skill therefore relies on reader-paced stepping, not on motion, and does not claim the stepped form teaches better than a well-made static diagram (SKILL.md section 1 says so).
- **Redundant text.** Mayer reports a median effect of 0.10 for on-screen text added to narration, with reversals under learner control. The skill's advice not to repeat text a view already shows is weak and conditional.
- **Decoration.** Tufte-style minimalism is contested (G4). The skill says omit by default and does not call decoration harmful.
- **Novice versus expert.** What helps novices (steps, pre-teaching, signals) can hurt experts (L3). The skill makes the reader choice the first decision instead of giving one set of rules.
- **Few-user testing.** NN/g limits its own claim to comparable users and says other methods need more people; the skill says to use a few readers over several rounds and gives no statistics.
- **Persistent state, explaining old and new values, text-only path, prediction prompts.** Supported by general principles (L6, A3, L2) rather than direct tests on explanations of this kind.
